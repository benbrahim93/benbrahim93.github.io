Keyboards and Mice: Stop Paying for Specs You Can't Feel

By Brahim Ben Sliman · Updated 2026-10-14 · 7 min read

Keyboard and mouse shopping is dominated by numbers that do not survive contact with real use. Polling rates in the thousands of hertz, sensors rated far beyond anything your desk needs, the colour of a switch you cannot even see. None of it tells you how the thing feels under your hands at nine in the morning.

Here is what actually matters for a home office or study setup, and where the spec sheet is quietly lying to you.

How much should you actually spend?

You do not need to spend much, and going up-market buys you feel and comfort, not performance.

  • £25–£50** — a perfectly good keyboard or mouse. Shape, size and switch feel matter far more than price here.
  • £50–£100** — better build, nicer keycaps, a mouse shaped for your hand. This is where the sensible money sits.
  • £100+** — often gamer branding and features you will never use. Rarely worth it for typing and spreadsheets.

A £30 well-shaped mouse beats a £90 gamer mouse for writing and spreadsheets every time. The expensive one is tuned for fast, flicking movements in games, not for an eight-hour workday.

The features that matter

Switches, by feel and not by colour. Ignore the colour names — they are brand shorthand and mean little across makers. What matters is three types. *Linear* switches move straight down with no bump: smooth and relatively quiet, good for fast typing. *Tactile* switches have a small bump at the point they register, so you feel when the key has fired; many people find this the nicest for typing. *Clicky* switches add an audible click on top of that bump. How to choose without trying them: if you type a lot and like feedback, pick tactile; if you want smooth and quiet, pick linear; pick clicky only if you work alone.

Clicky switches are a bad idea in a shared room. The noise carries, and it carries especially badly on a video call. In an office, a study shared with anyone, or an open-plan space, avoid clicky. It is the one spec that will actively annoy the people around you.

Layout and size. A full-size board includes a numeric keypad on the right. TKL, or tenkeyless, drops the keypad. 75% keeps most keys but squeezes them together. 60% throws away the function row, the arrow keys and the keypad. Each step down saves desk space and costs you keys. If your work involves typing numbers — accounts, data entry — keep the keypad or buy a separate one. Everyone else can usually live without it and gain the desk space back.

Keycaps. A cheap board with decent caps feels far better than a nice board wearing thin, shiny ones. Thicker caps feel more solid and less hollow. If a board feels rattly and cheap, better caps are the cheapest fix there is.

Wrist angle and keyboard height. The height at the back of the keyboard matters more than any backlight. A high rear edge tips your wrists up all day. A flatter board, with the little feet folded away, keeps your wrists neutral. RGB does nothing for your hands.

Mice: shape beats sensor. For office use the sensor is irrelevant; even basic ones track perfectly on a desk. What matters is the shape and size for your hand, the weight, how the scroll wheel feels, and whether the clicks are quiet. A mouse a little too small will make your hand ache by mid-afternoon, and no specification fixes that.

What to avoid

  • Buying on DPI or polling rate.** Office work does not come close to needing either.
  • Bluetooth for a desktop mouse if you can use 2.4GHz.** Bluetooth is fine for travel, but it can stutter and wake slowly. A 2.4GHz dongle is more reliable at a fixed desk; wired is the most reliable of all.
  • Wireless keyboards loaded with RGB.** The lighting eats the battery, and you will be charging it far more often than you would like.
  • Gamer styling over comfort.** Aggressive shapes and loud looks often compromise the very shape that made them comfortable.
  • Ignoring your hand size.** Check the mouse suits your hand before buying. Length and grip matter more than anything printed on the box.

Bottom line

Choose switches by feel, not colour. Avoid clicky in shared space. Decide whether you need a keypad before you shrink the layout. Buy a mouse shaped for your hand, ignore the sensor numbers, and choose 2.4GHz or wired over Bluetooth at a fixed desk. Spend in the middle band — that is where the comfort lives, and it lasts for years.
