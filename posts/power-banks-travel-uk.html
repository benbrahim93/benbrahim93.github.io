Power Banks: The Number That Actually Matters (and the Airline Rule)

Every power bank is sold on the same number: the milliamp-hours printed in big type on the box. It's the least useful figure on there. A 20,000mAh bank does not give your phone 20,000mAh of charge, and the gap isn't small. There's also a hard rule that catches people at the airport every summer.

How much should you actually spend?

  • Under £20** — a small top-up bank, around 10,000mAh. Fine for one phone charge on a day out. Expect a plastic body and no fast charging.
  • £20–£40** — the value band. A reputable 10,000–20,000mAh bank with USB-C power delivery. Most people should stop here.
  • £40–£70** — larger capacity and higher output for laptops, sometimes a display showing the exact percentage left.
  • £70+** — travel bricks for laptops and cameras. Buy one only if you genuinely carry the devices it's for.

The features that matter

Watt-hours, not milliamp-hours. Capacity is quoted in mAh at the cell voltage, typically 3.6V or 3.7V, while your phone charges at 5V and often higher. Converting is simple: mAh × volts ÷ 1000. A 10,000mAh bank is therefore about 37Wh. Then you lose something to the conversion — stepping a 3.7V cell up to 5V, plus heat and cable losses — so roughly two-thirds comes out the other end. A 10,000mAh bank typically delivers around 6,000–6,500mAh of usable charge, about one and a half charges of a modern phone. A 20,000mAh bank gets you roughly two to three charges, not four. That's why claims of "four charges from 20,000mAh" overstate it. Plan on two-thirds.

The airline rule. The figure that matters at the airport is watt-hours, not mAh. Most airlines allow banks up to 100Wh in hand baggage without asking — roughly 26,000–27,000mAh. Between 100Wh and 160Wh usually needs approval in advance, and above 160Wh is normally refused. Power banks must travel in cabin baggage, never in a checked bag, and some carriers restrict them further or cap how many you may carry. Check your own airline's page before you fly, and expect the rule to be applied at the gate as well as at security.

USB-C PD output. This is what charges a phone quickly. Without it, "fast charge" on the box often means fast for some other phone than yours.

PPS support, if you have a recent Android. It's an extension of USB-C PD that many Samsung and Pixel phones use for their top charging speed. If your phone wants it and the bank doesn't offer it, you'll charge slower and never know why.

Which ports you actually need. One USB-C in/out is the minimum — it charges the bank and your phone. A second port is handy for a second device, but ports you never use just add weight. Charging a laptop means checking output wattage against the laptop, not just capacity.

Pass-through charging means using the bank to charge your phone while the bank itself charges. Handy in a hotel with one socket, but not every bank does it, and some run warm. Look for it stated explicitly.

Cables matter more than people think. A long, thin cable can cut charging speed sharply and waste capacity as heat. Use a short, thick USB-C cable rated for the wattage you need, and treat the one in the box as a spare.

A percentage display beats four LED dots. It's the difference between "roughly full" and knowing you have enough for the trip home.

What to avoid

  • Unbranded cells.** The cheapest banks cut the protection circuitry that guards against overcharging and overheating. Not the place to save £8.
  • A swollen case.** A bank that's puffed up, won't sit flat, or stays warm when idle is finished. Stop using it, don't charge it, and take it to a household waste site that accepts batteries.
  • Capacity claims with no watt-hour figure.** If the box won't state Wh, buy a different box.
  • "Fast charge" with no protocol named.** It means nothing specific.
  • Huge capacity for a handbag.** A 20,000mAh bank weighs roughly as much as a small paperback and gets left at home.

By use case: a commute and a night out is 10,000mAh. A weekend away or a festival is 20,000mAh, and you'll want a second short cable. Travelling with a laptop means checking output wattage as carefully as capacity. Buy the cable with the bank.

Bottom line

Ignore the mAh headline, think in watt-hours, halve whatever you're promised, and check the bank states 100Wh or less in plain print before you take it near an airport. Buy USB-C PD at minimum, PPS if your phone wants it, and a branded unit from a seller you can return to. Then keep it out of the checked bag and charge it with a cable that can actually carry the power.
