Robot Vacuums: How Much You Need to Spend for the Thing to Be Worth It

Robot vacuums have a reputation problem, and most of it comes from people who bought the cheapest one they could find and expected it to clean a whole house while they were at work. The cheap end does work — just not like that.

The single decision that matters most is navigation. Suction numbers, app features and brand badges all come second. Get navigation wrong and no amount of extra spending fixes it.

How much should you actually spend?

Robot vacuum pricing falls into bands that line up almost exactly with navigation, which is the honest way to read them:

  • Under £150** — random-bounce navigation, often no app. These bump around a room, cover most of the floor eventually, and miss spots. Fine as a daily tidy-up in one open room. Not a whole-house cleaner.
  • Roughly £150–£300** — real navigation, using either a camera or a spinning lidar sensor. It maps your rooms, cleans in straight lines, and remembers where it has been. This is the band where a robot vacuum stops being a gadget and starts being useful.
  • Roughly £300–£500** — adds a self-emptying base, better brush design and stronger object avoidance. Convenience, mostly.
  • Above that** — you're paying for the base, app polish and the name on the box. The cleaning itself is not dramatically different from the band below.

Prices move constantly, so treat those bands as guides rather than gospel. What doesn't move is the order of importance.

The features that matter

Navigation, above everything. Random bounce versus mapping is the real dividing line. A mapping robot cleans the same floor in less time with fewer missed patches, and it can be told to do one room. If you have more than one room and any furniture at all, mapping is the feature to pay for.

Suction in pascals — mostly marketing. Brands quote pascals because the number gets bigger every year. Real cleaning depends far more on how long the brush passes over the dirt. A well-navigated 2,000 Pa robot out-cleans an aimless 5,000 Pa one in a normal home.

Brush type and hair tangling. Bristle brush rolls wrap long hair and pet fur into tight knots you have to cut out. Rubber or combo rollers shed hair far more easily. If anyone in the house has long hair, check the brush design before the suction figure.

Self-emptying base — useful, not essential. It means you empty the robot's bin weekly instead of daily. Worth it if it runs every day and you hate the chore. On a tight budget, skip the base and put the money into navigation.

Hard floors versus carpet. Robots are at their best on hard floors — they sweep crumbs and pet hair well. On deep carpet they only lift surface dust, and on high-pile carpet many simply can't climb on reliably. If carpet is your main floor, set expectations low.

Thresholds and doorways. Measure the lip on any doorway or rug edge you want it to cross. Cheap units get stuck. Better ones climb roughly 2 cm.

App or no app. An app gives you schedules, room selection and no-go zones. Without one you get a button and hope. For a single-room tidy-up the app barely matters. For a whole floor it matters a lot.

What to avoid

  • Buying on the pascal number alone.** It tells you the least about real cleaning.
  • A cheap random-bounce unit for a whole house.** This is the single most common bad buy.
  • Assuming it replaces your vacuum.** A robot is maintenance cleaning. You'll still want a corded or stick vacuum for stairs, edges and spills.
  • Budget mopping.** A damp cloth dragged around is not mopping. If you want a mop, buy a mop.
  • Ignoring clearance.** It has to fit under your sofa and bed, or the dust under there stays put.
  • Pet hair on the cheapest hardware.** It's the fastest way to clog a brush and wear out a cheap motor.

Read the specs for navigation type, brush design and clearance height, and ignore the suction headline. If you're torn between a robot and a stick, the stick will serve a cluttered or carpeted home better for the same money.

Bottom line

Under £150, a robot vacuum is a single-room tidier and nothing more. Spend into the roughly £150–£300 mapping band and it becomes a genuinely useful appliance that runs while you're out. The self-emptying base is a convenience upgrade, not a requirement.

If your home is mostly carpet, heavily cluttered, or has one room worth cleaning, a decent cordless stick vacuum will do more for less money. Buy the robot for hard floors and open space. Buy the stick for everything else.
