Espresso Machines Under £200: Where the Money Should Go

Espresso machines under £200 are where most people start, and where most people quietly give up. The machine is rarely the problem. The problem is the order the money gets spent in, and the fact that nobody tells you the grinder matters more than the machine.

Here is the honest version of how to spend £200 on home espresso, so you end up with coffee you keep making instead of a machine that lives in a cupboard by spring.

How much should you actually spend?

There is one rule that saves more bad buys than any other: the grinder comes first. A good grinder paired with a modest machine makes better espresso than a fancy machine fed on stale pre-ground coffee. Freshly ground, correctly sized coffee is most of the flavour.

So if your budget is £200, a sane split is roughly £100–£130 on the machine and £70–£100 on a burr grinder. If that means you cannot afford both today, buy the grinder now and use a moka pot or filter until you can. That is not a downgrade — it is the faster route to coffee you actually enjoy.

Price bands, plainly:

  • Under £100** — manual lever machines and simple thermoblock units. Capable, but you do more of the work.
  • £100–£200** — the sensible middle. Better temperature control, a real steam wand, room to grow into.
  • Over £200** — out of scope here, and rarely necessary to make good coffee at home.

The features that matter

Temperature stability. Espresso is unforgiving about heat. A machine that swings hot and cold gives you a sour shot one minute and a bitter one the next, with nothing changed on your side. This is the single feature worth more than any other.

A real steam wand, not a panarello sleeve. The little plastic cuff that froths milk by blasting air into it makes stiff, bubbly foam. A bare wand lets you texture milk properly — silky, pourable, the thing that turns a shot into a flat white. Milk texturing is also the skill that decides whether you keep using the machine.

Pressurised versus non-pressurised baskets. Pressurised baskets (sometimes called dual-wall) use a tiny valve to fake the pressure a proper grind would create. They are forgiving with coarse or pre-ground coffee and easy to start with, but they cap how good the coffee can get. Non-pressurised baskets are honest: they need a good grind and a bit of technique, and they reward both. Buy a machine that ships with both if you can, learn on the pressurised one, then move across.

Machine type, in plain terms. Manual lever machines give you full control and cost little, but you supply the pressure by hand and it takes practice. Thermoblock machines heat water on demand — quick to warm up, with less thermal mass, so temperature can wander between shots. Single-boiler machines share one boiler between brewing and steaming, so you brew, wait, then steam. None of these is wrong. They are different amounts of work for different amounts of money.

And it is not a pod machine. Capsule machines are a different product with different running costs. The hardware is cheap and the pods are not — you pay for convenience by the cup, forever, and you are tied to one supply. That is a fair choice, but it is not the same decision as buying an espresso machine, so do not compare the two on the price of the box.

What to avoid

  • Skipping the grinder.** Blade grinders chop unevenly and will not do espresso. A burr grinder is not optional.
  • No tamper that fits, and no scales.** A tamper the wrong size, and no way to weigh your dose, means you are guessing every shot. A correctly sized tamper and a cheap set of scales are non-negotiables.
  • Ignoring your water.** In a hard-water area, scale kills machines. If you will not descale and backflush, and would rather not filter your water, buy something simpler.
  • Paying for chrome.** Shiny cases do not make coffee. Spend on temperature control and the wand.

Bottom line

Spend the money in the right order: grinder, then scale, then machine, then tamper. Insist on temperature stability and a real steam wand. Learn on a pressurised basket if you like, then move to non-pressurised. And be honest about maintenance — if you will not descale, backflush and look after the water, a moka pot or a good filter setup will make you happier coffee for a fraction of the fuss. A machine you will not maintain is money wasted.
