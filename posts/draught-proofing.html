Draught-Proofing: What Pays for It and What Is Just Tape

By Brahim Ben Sliman · Updated 2026-10-14 · 6 min read

Every house loses heat in two ways: through ventilation it needs, and through gaps it doesn't. Draught-proofing is about the second kind. The aim is to stop paying the boiler to warm the street one letterbox-shaped hole at a time. Done well, the whole materials bill comes to less than a takeaway and the skill required is noticing moving air with the back of your hand. Here's the payback ladder, what to buy for each gap, and where to stop.

How much should you actually spend?

The payback ladder in ascending cost — which is roughly the payback order, cheapest first:

  • Letterbox brush flap and keyhole cover — a few pounds.** A letterbox is a hole in your front door with a gale behind it. Two screws, ten minutes.
  • Brush sweep for the bottom of exterior doors — a similar few pounds.** The gap under a front or back door is often a house's biggest draught.
  • Sealing strips for windows and door frames — roughly £5 to £20 per window.** Which type matters, below.
  • Chimney balloon or pillow — roughly £20 to £30.** In many houses an unused chimney is the biggest single loss in the building — warm air drawn up all winter, like a window left on the latch.
  • Loft hatch and pipe penetrations — pennies to a few pounds.** Two leaks everyone forgets.
  • Door curtain or thermal curtains — the most expensive item here,** but the blunt answer for a hopeless old door or single glazing: a curtain cuts air movement and insulates at once.

All of it together usually comes to under roughly £100; a paid survey can wait until you've felt the difference yourself.

The features that matter

Choosing the right strip. Self-adhesive foam tape is the cheapest and the shortest-lived — it squashes flat in a season or two and the adhesive lets go on cold, damp frames. Use it only for small, even gaps, and expect to redo it. Brush strips cope with uneven gaps and sliding sashes. Compression bulb strips — the rubber type, usually EPDM — seal best and last for years, but they need a groove or screws. Match the thickness to the gap: too thick and the window won't shut; too thin and you've bought decoration.

Sash windows. Sashes leak along the beads that hold the panes and at the meeting rail. A strip fitted to the sash edges closes most of it without butchering the window; a full overhaul is worth pricing against a good thermal curtain.

Chimneys. If the fireplace is never used, a chimney balloon or pillow stops the flue drawing warm air out around the clock. Two rules: take it out before any fire is ever lit, and never block a flue serving a working appliance.

Loft hatches and pipes. Treat the hatch like a door and strip its edges. Then hunt the small ones: pipes behind the boiler, under the sinks, into the airing cupboard — a bead of silicone or foam per hole.

The vents that must stay open. Trickle vents in window frames, wall vents and airbricks are designed ventilation, not faults. Leave them be — blocking them trades a draught for damp walls.

Curtains. A door curtain on a draughty front door, or thermal curtains over single glazing, is blunt but effective: it slows the air and the cold radiating off the glass. Strips for the gaps, curtains where the window itself is the problem.

Finding draughts without a thermal camera. The back of your hand on a windy evening, swept slowly along frames, skirting boards and exterior doors, finds most of it. An incense stick betrays leaks as wavering smoke; a tissue on string does the same at corners. Test on the windiest day you get — on a calm one, the gaps hide.

What to avoid

  • Over-sealing the house.** Homes are built to breathe. Seal everything and the moisture from cooking, showering and breathing lands on the coldest surfaces — streaming windows, then mould in the corners. If that starts, reopen a vent.
  • Blocking airbricks, trickle vents or anything serving a boiler or a used fireplace.** Those aren't leaks; they're requirements.
  • Fancy door furniture.** A brass handle stops nothing; the strip under the door does the work.
  • Novelty draught-excluder animals.** They slide out of position and cover an inch of a foot-long gap; a brush sweep does the same job properly.

Buy strips after you've put a hand to each gap and judged its width; people buy the wrong thickness and end up with windows that won't close. Beyond that, it's a screwdriver, a Sunday and the discipline to stop.

Bottom line

Find the gaps with your hand on the windiest evening. Seal the unused chimney, brush under the exterior doors, cover the letterbox, strip the loft hatch, then do the windows and a curtain where the glass is the problem. Keep every designed vent open, and stop before the house feels sealed. Under roughly £100 and a weekend, most of the payback sits in the first three rungs of the ladder; the cheap fixes pay.
