Dehumidifiers: Compressor or Desiccant, and What Size Your Room Needs

By Brahim Ben Sliman · Updated 2026-10-14 · 6 min read

A dehumidifier is the one appliance whose whole appeal is a bucket filling up with water. Every litre in there is moisture that used to be sitting in your walls, your windows and your washing. Get the choice right and the condensation on the bedroom windows stops, the black specks behind the wardrobe stop spreading, and laundry hung indoors dries overnight instead of hanging there for two days. Get it wrong and you've paid for a box that hums in the corner and fills a quarter of a bucket a week. Fix the type and the size first — everything else follows.

How much should you actually spend?

Dehumidifiers split into three price bands, and the step up between them buys convenience and capacity, not a different result in your hallway:

  • Under £50** — mini units for a wardrobe or a single small bedroom; they will not dry a damp hallway.
  • Roughly £50–£120** — the sensible band for most UK homes: 10–20 litre-class compressor machines or mid-size desiccants with a humidistat and laundry mode. Start here; you'll probably finish here.
  • Roughly £120–£250 and up** — bigger tanks, quieter builds, continuous-drainage kits as standard. Justified for open-plan ground floors; not for one damp bedroom.

What extra money doesn't buy is extraction: the litres-per-day figure is the same marketing physics at every price, and a cheap unit in the right corner out-dries an expensive one placed badly. Before spending, leave a hygrometer in the worst room for a week: 60% or higher for whole days is a real damp problem; below 50%, buy nothing.

The features that matter

Compressor or desiccant — pick by the room's temperature. Compressor units are the standard choice: cheaper to run in the warm, normally heated parts of a house. They're bulkier and noisier, and they struggle in unheated garages, sheds and outhouses, where the coil ices up and extraction collapses. Desiccant units work in cold rooms, are lighter and often quieter, and blow the air back out slightly warmed — noticeable on a cold morning. Desiccants generally use more electricity for the same water. Unheated space, desiccant; everywhere else, compressor.

The litres-per-day figure is a lab number — size by rooms instead. Extraction ratings are measured in a closed room held near 30°C and near saturation. A British lounge is nothing like that, so a "20 litre" machine pulls a fraction of it in real use. Match the unit to the space: small-to-mid for a bedroom or a hall, larger for an open-plan living area.

Continuous drainage beats bucket-emptying. A machine doing its job fills the bucket daily, twice daily in a cold wet snap, and a full bucket switches it off. If it will live near a drain or a low sink, a hose turns it into a set-and-forget appliance.

Laundry mode is the feature that gets used most. Hang the washing, shut the door, and the clothes dry in a few hours instead of a day and a half, with the moisture captured in the tank rather than on the window frames. It's also the clearest money-saver: a clothes horse costs nothing, while a tumble dryer load burns several kilowatt-hours. Put both through the same sums — appliance watts × hours × your tariff.

Set a target, and buy a hygrometer. Roughly 60–65% humidity is damp-ish; the low 50s is comfortable. A humidistat set in the low 50s switches the machine off rather than running it round the clock, and a hygrometer — a couple of pounds, the most useful accessory here — tells you whether it's earning its keep.

Noise, if it's going in a bedroom. Fan speed dominates; compressor units hum and click as they cycle. Check the decibel figure, run the low setting after bedtime, or keep the unit downstairs with the door shut overnight. They all run off an ordinary three-pin socket, so placement is free.

What to avoid

  • Sizing by the litres figure.** Measured in a warm, sealed, near-saturated room — not a cool UK bedroom.
  • Peltier "mini" dehumidifiers for real rooms.** They collect cupfuls; fine for a wardrobe, useless for a hallway.
  • Running it with the door open or by an open window.** You'd be drying the street. Near the moisture source, doors closed.
  • Paying premium prices to fix placement.** Price doesn't fix it; placement does.
  • Assuming you need one at all.** If the windows fog while you cook and clear afterwards, the cooker hood is the fix.

Bottom line

Decide compressor versus desiccant by how cold the room gets. Size by rooms, not litres. Fit continuous drainage if you can, lean on laundry mode, set the humidistat in the low 50s, and keep the doors shut while it runs. Used like that, a roughly £50–£120 machine does everything a £250 one will. If a hygrometer shows rooms already comfortable, buy nothing.
