Heated Clothes Airers: Are They Actually Cheaper Than a Tumble Dryer?

By Brahim Ben Sliman · Updated 2026-10-14 · 6 min read

Winter turns laundry into a problem: the line becomes decorative, the radiators wear socks and smalls, and the tumble dryer becomes one of the hungriest appliances in the house. A heated clothes airer promises the same result for a fraction of the power. The advertising treats that as the whole story.

The honest version: a heated airer is genuinely cheap to run — a small heater warming rails rather than a big one blasting a drum. But it dries slowly, tips the moisture from your washing into the room, and is hopeless against a full bath-towel load. Whether it beats your tumble dryer depends on what you dry, how much, and what you pay per unit of electricity. Here's how to work it out without trusting anyone's headline number, including mine.

How much should you actually spend?

Three bands, and the extra money buys capacity and control, not shortcuts to dry towels:

  • Roughly £30–£50** — basic tiered or flat racks: a low-wattage element in the rails, no cover, no timer. Fine for a few shirts and school uniforms; undersized for a family wash.
  • Roughly £50–£110** — winged designs, usually sold with a cover: hanging space for shirts plus flat tiers for towels. The sensible band for most households.
  • Roughly £110–£180** — enclosed fan-assisted dryers: a fabric jacket around the rack, like a gentle warm marquee. Moving air dries faster than static warmth, so these cope with big loads best.

The top band isn't faster drying — a bare 200-watt rack takes as long as it takes. Pay for wings, a timer and a thermostat, then stop.

The features that matter

The maths — do it your way, once. A tumble dryer pushes a high-wattage element — most draw a couple of thousand watts or more — flat out for the whole cycle; a heated airer runs a low-wattage element, typically between 150 and 300 watts, for hours. Cost either way is watts ÷ 1,000 × hours × your pence-per-kWh. Read the wattage off the plate, your unit rate off the latest bill, and put the two side by side. Usually that makes the airer a few pence a load and the dryer several times that — a heat-pump dryer narrows the gap.

The wings. Extending rails on folding arms, so shirts and dresses hang apart instead of folded over one bar — air reaches every garment, so things dry faster and crease less. The biggest capacity upgrade going. If you hang most of your wash, buy wings.

Tiered versus angled. Flat tiers suit towels and bedding; angled or hanging sections suit shirts fed straight from the machine. Tiered-only racks feel too small on laundry day, because one towel eats a whole tier.

A cover. A zip-up jacket traps the warm layer around the load and roughly halves drying time. It earns respect: never run a covered airer unattended or overnight, keep bedding and curtains well clear, and rely on a timer or the cut-out, not memory.

Timer and thermostat — the real cost controls. The cheapest electricity is the unit you don't use. Four hours with a timer, then finish in still air, beats eight hours out of habit.

Room for your biggest wash. Size for the largest load you actually run, not the light wash you do on Tuesdays. A two-person flat manages on a tiered rack; a family of four wants wings, a cover and preferably the fan-assisted type.

The catch the adverts skip. Indoors, the water has to go somewhere. A full load gives off a couple of litres as it dries, and in a small flat that reads as fogged windows by teatime. Ventilate the room or run a dehumidifier alongside — an airer that dries the clothes while the walls go damp hasn't saved you anything.

What to avoid

  • Expecting towel-speed drying.** No spin and modest heat mean bath towels and thick denim take an age. Fine overnight; maddening if you wanted them by lunch.
  • Sealed little rooms.** An airer in a shut bathroom fills the room with water. Door open or window cracked, always.
  • Running it all day "just in case".** The timer exists for a reason.
  • Clothes draped directly on radiators.** Slower drying, scorch marks, and a humidity spike that ends up on your windows. A clothes horse beside a radiator beats it.

Bottom line

For regular indoor drying, a heated airer with a cover and a timer usually costs far less per load than a conventional tumble dryer and is kinder to wool, elastic and anything that shrinks. If you dry rarely, or the heating is on anyway and a clothes horse beside the radiator does the job, keep your money — that's the cheapest option of all. Do the sum with your own tariff, give the moisture somewhere to go, and stretch to a fan-assisted dryer only if the towel pile is genuinely weekly.
