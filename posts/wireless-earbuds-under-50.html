Wireless Earbuds Under £50: What You Give Up, and What You Don't

Cheap true-wireless earbuds are one of the best value buys in tech. The hard engineering — tiny drivers, Bluetooth, a charging case — got cheap years ago. What stayed expensive is the clever extras: noise cancelling that genuinely works, microphone processing that copes with wind, and app-based EQ.

So a sub-£50 pair isn't a broken version of a £180 pair. It's the same basic job with the extras stripped out. Which changes the question, from "are cheap earbuds any good?" to "which extras do I actually use?"

How much should you actually spend?

  • Under £25** — a spare pair, or podcasts in a quiet room. Expect short battery life and a case that feels hollow.
  • £25–£50** — the value band. Decent fit, comfortable for a couple of hours, clear calls in a quiet room, and roughly a working day between case charges.
  • £50–£90** — better microphones, several days of case charge, and often multipoint pairing, so the earbuds follow you between a phone and a laptop.
  • £150+** — noise cancelling that quiets a train, microphone processing that survives a windy street, and app-based EQ.

What you don't lose by spending less is the music itself. Sound quality here is mostly about seal, not driver specs. A £40 pair that fits your ears well will usually sound fuller and clearer than a £180 pair that doesn't.

The features that matter

Fit and seal, before anything else. A good seal gives you bass, blocks background noise and lets you listen at lower volumes. A weak seal means thin sound and tired ears. Try every tip size in the box, then buy a pack of replacement tips if none quite work. They cost a few pounds and beat spending another £40 on the earbuds.

Battery, split into two numbers. The box quotes one big figure, but it's usually buds plus case. A "30 hour" pair might mean 5 hours in the buds and 25 in the case. The buds are what matter on a commute; the case is just the refill. And cells this small age quickly — after a year of daily use expect a noticeable drop, especially if you leave the case charged in a warm bag.

Calls, indoors versus outdoors. Sub-£50 microphones are clear at a desk or in a kitchen. On a windy street or a busy station they struggle, and the noise reduction chops your voice in and out. If most of your calls happen outdoors, spend on the microphone and accept plainer sound.

Latency, for video. On a phone, cheap earbuds are usually fine because the handset negotiates a low-latency mode. On a laptop, lip-sync can drift — annoying for YouTube, useless for editing to a beat. Test with a video in the first week and return them if it bothers you.

Water resistance, plainly put. IPX4 means sweat and splashes are fine: gym use, a rainy walk, a spilled drink. IPX7 means the earbuds survive being dunked briefly. It doesn't mean you can swim in them, and says nothing about the case, which usually gets soaked.

The case matters as much as the buds. It's the battery, the on/off switch and the storage all in one. Look for USB-C rather than micro-USB, enough capacity that you charge it weekly, and a size that actually lives in a pocket.

Multipoint pairing, if you switch between a laptop and a phone all day. It's typically a £50-plus feature, but the gap is narrowing.

What to avoid

  • Noise cancelling as the headline at this price.** Budget ANC dulls a hum and adds a faint hiss. It isn't silence.
  • Micro-USB on anything.** A sign the product was designed years ago and left on the shelf.
  • Brandless marketplace listings** with glowing specs and vague names. No support, no returns worth having.
  • Touch controls if you wear a hat six months a year.** Physical buttons are more reliable in cold weather.
  • Buying on driver size.** A bigger number doesn't mean better sound. Seal does.

Bottom line

Shop the £25–£50 band, check the case charges over USB-C and fits in your pocket, and weight in-bud battery above the total on the box. Get the seal right and you'll get most of what a much dearer pair gives you. If your calls are mostly outdoors, or you fly often and want real quiet, save for the mid-range instead.

Or buy wired. If you're mostly at a desk or on a train and want the most sound for your money, a £25 wired pair beats a £25 wireless pair and never needs charging. Wireless is convenience. Don't pay for convenience you won't use.
