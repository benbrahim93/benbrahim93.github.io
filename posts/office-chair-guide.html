Ergonomic Office Chairs: What Your Back Actually Needs for £150

By Brahim Ben Sliman · Updated 2026-10-14 · 6 min read

If you work from home and your lower back has opinions by mid-afternoon, the chair is the first suspect, often rightly. But the word ergonomic costs a manufacturer nothing: no rule stops a £45 chair with a plastic bump on the backrest printing it in bold. A real ergonomic chair differs from an office-shaped seat in the parts that move — a chair fits a body by adjusting, not by looking scientific.

That list is what you are buying, plus one caveat the box never prints: the chair is roughly a third of your setup. Desk height and monitor position matter just as much.

How much should you actually spend?

  • Under £60** — chairs that go up and down, usually the whole adjustment list. Fine for an hour or two a day; the word ergonomic here is decoration.
  • £80–£250** — the band where real adjustments appear: moving lumbar support, moving armrests, recline with a tension dial. This is where a £150 budget lands.
  • Above £250** — better materials, heavier frames, longer warranties. The adjustment list often looks identical to a good £150 chair.

Spend in the middle band, then judge it against the checklist below.

The features that matter

Seat height, the baseline. Nearly every task chair has it; the range matters more. Feet flat, thighs roughly level. Check the minimum height if you are shorter, the maximum if taller.

Adjustable lumbar support. A fixed bump fits one spine, probably not yours. Support that moves up and down, ideally in and out, sits in the curve at the small of your back. Depth beats bulk.

Recline with tension control. Bolt upright all day is not what backs want. A tilting backrest with a tension dial set to your bodyweight splits real ergonomic chairs from cheap copies, which lock solid or flop backwards.

Armrests, 2D at least, 4D ideally. The one job is forearms level with the desk, shoulders relaxed. Height adjustment is the minimum; 4D arms matter because elbows belong near your ribs, not flared out to reach arms mounted wide.

Seat depth. Leave two or three fingers of space between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knees. Too deep digs in; too shallow loads the thighs at the edge. Sliding-depth seats are worth hunting down if you are far from average height.

Mesh or padding. Mesh breathes and wins on warm afternoons; padding feels firmer. The weak point of cheap mesh is not the mesh but the plastic frame stretched over it, which cracks at stress points after a couple of years. Buy mesh from the middle band up, where frames are thicker.

Foam density. The classic cheap-chair failure is flat foam by month six. Thin padding feels fine in the showroom and is pressed solid after a season of full days. Look for high-density foam, and trust six-month reviews over unboxing reactions.

Weight rating and fit. Domestic task chairs typically carry a rating around 110 to 150 kg; exceed it and frames or gas lifts fail early. A chair built large can also be too deep for a smaller person, so check minimum seat height and width too.

The chair is one third of the setup. Feet flat, or on a cheap footrest if the desk is high and your feet dangle. Forearms level with the desk. Top of the screen at or just below eye height, an arm's length away. A good chair under a laptop lying flat on a table fixes nothing.

What to avoid

  • The word instead of the adjustments.** If a listing at any price offers seat height and nothing else, it is an office-shaped chair whatever the badge says.
  • The permanent dining chair.** A dining chair plus a cushion is genuinely acceptable for a short stretch — a week of illness, a borrowed desk. As a daily workstation it is a mistake you pay for.
  • Judging foam from unboxing reviews.** Every seat feels plump on day one. The verdict that counts is written in month six.
  • Buying for looks.** Racing-style buckets photograph well and hold you in one rigid position, with bolsters you cannot lean against.

Chairs arrive flat-packed and take the best part of an hour with the Allen key, so leave the time. The warranty is a marker of frame confidence: five years on the frame means the maker expects it to last five; a guarantee counted in months on a daily chair is information.

Bottom line

Count the five adjustments — height, lumbar, recline, arms, depth — and buy in the £80–£250 band, where they exist together. Choose mesh or padding by how warm you run. Then fix the other two thirds of the setup: the best chair is still one third of the answer, and a footrest or monitor riser often helps more than a new chair would.
