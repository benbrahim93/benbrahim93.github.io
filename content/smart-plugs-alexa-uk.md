Smart plugs are the cheapest way into a smart home, and the most common place people quietly give up. The failure is almost never the plug itself. It is pairing, socket fit, or expecting the plug to do something it cannot.

Here is how to buy the right ones and get them working first time.

## How much should you actually spend?

Smart plugs cluster into a few bands, and the differences are protocol and reliability, not magic:

- **Under £10 each** — Wi-Fi plugs from lesser-known brands. They work, but app support can vanish and they can be fiddly to pair.
- **£10–£20 each** — the sensible middle. Wi-Fi or hub-based plugs from brands that stick around, with energy monitoring on some models.
- **£20+ each** — hub or Thread plugs, often sold in kits. You are paying for a more reliable network, not a better plug.

## The features that matter

**The protocol, in plain terms.** *Wi-Fi plugs* connect straight to your router — nothing else needed, easiest to set up, but each one is a device on your network and a houseful of them starts to crowd it. *Zigbee and Thread plugs* talk to a hub or a newer smart speaker over a low-power network, so they do not load your Wi-Fi, but you need the hub and there is more setup. *Bluetooth-only plugs* work over a short range and usually cannot be controlled when you are out. For most UK homes, Wi-Fi plugs are the simplest place to start; go hub-based once you know you are adding a lot of devices.

**2.4GHz only — the single most common failure.** Almost all Wi-Fi smart plugs use the 2.4GHz band and cannot see a 5GHz network. If your phone or router is handing out 5GHz during setup, pairing fails with no useful error. The fix: make sure your phone is on the 2.4GHz band, or switch 5GHz off for a few minutes while you pair. This one thing solves more "it will not connect" problems than anything else.

**Plug size and the second socket.** On a UK double socket, a fat smart plug can block the socket beside it. Measure, and look for a slim design if you need both sockets usable. It is a real annoyance the product photos rarely show.

**UK three-pin reality.** UK plugs need an earth pin, and a smart plug has to pass that through properly. Some slim or imported designs cut corners, so stick to plugs made for UK sockets rather than adapters.

**Power rating.** Check the maximum load, in amps or watts. Plugs are often rated around 13A, but not all are, and some cheap ones are lower. Heaters, kettles and microwaves draw high current and are not safe on every plug. Never use a smart plug on anything that could overheat, or that you leave running unattended.

## What to avoid

- **Expecting dimming or speed control.** A smart plug is on or off. It cannot dim a lamp, change a fan's speed, or do anything gradual. For those you need a smart bulb or a smart fan.
- **Devices whose own switch you leave off.** If the device's physical switch is off, the plug has no power to give. Plugs control the supply, not the button on the appliance.
- **Cheap brands that vanish from the app.** Some budget plugs stop working when the maker drops support or shuts down its server. Avoid being stranded by sticking to brands you can still buy in a year, and by preferring plugs that also work with a mainstream assistant.

**Energy monitoring.** This is the genuinely useful feature. A plug that reports how much a device uses lets you find the real running cost of that old fridge or heater — often the reason to buy smart plugs in the first place.

**Scheduling and away mode.** Turning lamps on and off on a schedule is the classic, genuinely useful trick, and it does not need a hub. It is the automation most people actually use every day.

**What deserves automating.** The honest answer: lamps, a charging routine, a fan in summer, and energy monitoring. That is most of it. Beyond that, automating things you would have to walk over and switch on anyway saves nothing — a smart plug on the kettle is a novelty, because you still have to fill it and press the button. Buy plugs for the jobs you will genuinely stop doing, not for the idea of a smart home.

> One honest note: I am writing this as a researched buying guide — the protocol, socket-fit and power advice here is what decides whether smart plugs work reliably, and that is what matters. Check the current model range before you buy.

## Bottom line

Wi-Fi plugs for simplicity, hub-based if you are building a bigger network. Pair on 2.4GHz and almost every setup failure disappears. Check the plug clears your double socket and that its power rating suits the device. Use them for lamps, schedules and energy monitoring, and skip the gimmicks. And buy from a brand that will still be here next year.
