Every electric heater works the same way underneath. A resistive element turns electricity into heat, and you pay for every kilowatt-hour it draws. There is no clever heater that makes a room warm for less electricity — only heaters that run for less time. Understanding that one fact saves you money before you've bought anything.

So the real question isn't "which heater is cheapest to run?" It's "which heater warms the room I'm in, and how do I stop it running when I don't need it?"

## How much should you actually spend?

Accept this first: the price of the heater is the cheap part. A roughly £25 convector and a roughly £90 oil-filled radiator are about as expensive to run as each other, because they turn electricity into heat the same way. What you spend buys how the heat feels and how well it's controlled — not cheaper heat.

- **Roughly £20–£40** — basic convector heaters. Light, loud-ish, quick to warm a small room, no storage of heat.
- **Roughly £40–£90** — oil-filled radiators and better panel heaters. Slower to warm up, quieter, and they keep giving out heat for a while after switching off.
- **Roughly £30–£80** — infrared and halogen heaters. They warm people and surfaces rather than the air, and feel instant when you're sitting in front of them.
- **Whatever you pay, add a plug-in timer or a thermostat.** That's the part that actually saves money.

The running cost dwarfs the purchase price, so choose the type that suits your room and how you use it.

## The features that matter

**The running-cost maths.** A kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts. A 2kW heater uses 2 kilowatt-hours (kWh) every hour it's on full. Your cost is roughly: **kW × hours × your unit rate**. A 2kW heater on full for four hours uses about 8kWh. Multiply that by the pence-per-kWh figure on your bill — check your current tariff, because it varies — and you have your answer.

**Why 2kW versus 3kW is not the whole story.** A 3kW heater only costs more if it runs longer or harder. It warms a cold room faster, so it may spend less time at full power. A 2kW heater in the same room may just sit at full power for longer. Match wattage to room size and both can end up similar.

**The "efficient heater" claim.** All resistive electric heaters convert essentially all the electricity they draw into heat — close to 100% in every one of them. So when a heater is sold as efficient, it usually means controllable, not thrifty. The thermostat and timer are what cut the bill.

**Thermostat and timer — the real cost controls.** A thermostat switches the heater off once the room reaches your set temperature, so a 2kW heater rarely runs flat out for hours. A timer means it runs only when the room is used. Together they do more for your bill than any heater design.

**Convection, oil-filled, infrared, halogen.** Convectors push warm air around the room and heat the whole space. Oil-filled radiators do the same but gently, staying warm after you switch off. Infrared and halogen heat you and nearby objects directly, so they feel instant but only work if you sit near them. Pick for the room.

**Small occupied room versus the whole house.** Heating the one room you're sitting in nearly always costs less than warming the whole house if the rest stays cold. That's a lifestyle decision as much as a heating one.

## What to avoid

- **Plugging a heater into an extension lead.** High-wattage heaters should go straight into a wall socket. Extension leads and multi-way adaptors can overheat.
- **Blocking the heater.** Keep it clear of curtains, bedding and furniture, and never dry washing draped over it.
- **Running a heater to fight damp and condensation.** Ventilation fixes damp. A heater just sits under the moisture problem.
- **Leaving it on high all night with no timer.** It's the most expensive way to use one.
- **Believing a cheaper-to-run electric heater exists.** Between resistive heaters, there isn't one.

## Bottom line

The heater you choose barely changes the running cost. All resistive electric heaters are about as efficient as each other, and the bill is set by how many kilowatt-hours you use — kW × hours × your unit rate. So buy for the room: a heater that warms a small occupied space well, run on a thermostat and a timer, beats one that quietly tries to heat the whole house. Check your current tariff before you work out the numbers, and put the money into control rather than clever wording on the box.
