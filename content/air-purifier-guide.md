An air purifier is a fan pushing air through a very fine filter. It's genuinely good at exactly one thing: catching airborne particles. Dust, pollen, pet dander and smoke residue all get pulled through and trapped. Equally important is what it doesn't do. It does nothing about humidity, won't dry out a damp room, and barely touches gases and odours unless it also carries an activated carbon filter — which many don't, or only fit as a thin token layer. It doesn't cure anything, either.

So the first step isn't shopping, it's naming the problem. If your problem is particles in the air, a well-chosen purifier genuinely helps. If it's damp, smells or a health condition, spend the money fixing that instead, because this machine can't.

## How much should you actually spend?

Purifier pricing falls into three bands, and the value sits in the middle:

- **Roughly £40–£80** — small units built for a bedroom or study. One filter, basic controls. Genuinely fine if that's your room.
- **Roughly £80–£180** — the sensible middle. More airflow, quieter low speeds, and filters that are easy to find. This is where most homes should shop.
- **Roughly £180–£350** — large-room capacity and nicer design. You're paying for throughput and looks, not fundamentally better filtration.

The £300 unit and the £80 unit can carry the same core filter type. What the extra money buys is airflow and a nicer app. That matters in a big open-plan room; for a bedroom it usually doesn't.

## The features that matter

**The HEPA wording, read carefully.** "HEPA-type", "HEPA-like" and "HEPA-style" are marketing phrases with no fixed meaning — they can describe a loose mesh that catches far less. Look for "true HEPA" or an equivalent certified filter. If a listing leans on the vague versions and never states the real specification plainly, treat the evasion as your answer.

**Room size, matched honestly.** The spec that matters is CADR — clean air delivery rate, how much filtered air the unit moves per hour. Compare the maker's stated room size with your real room; if you sit between two sizes, buy the bigger. An undersized purifier runs at full speed permanently: maximum noise, and still losing ground. Room-size matching beats anything printed on the box.

**Filter prices, checked before you buy.** Running costs have two halves, and people only think about one. The electricity is small — purifiers draw modest wattage, often similar to an old light bulb. The real cost is filters, typically replaced every six to twelve months at wildly varying prices. Check replacement filter prices and UK availability before buying anything. A sealed unit whose filters are rare or expensive is a subscription with a free gift at the start, and a cheaper machine with affordable filters beats an expensive one on refills every time.

**Noise, especially at low speed.** In a bedroom you'll run it quiet for most of the night. A sleep mode that dims the display and drops to a whisper is a genuine feature. Compare the low-speed noise figure, not the maximum.

**Placement.** A bedroom suits a small, quiet unit near the bed with the door shut. A large living room needs a bigger machine running for longer — and a purifier tucked behind a sofa mostly filters the space behind the sofa. Leave a clear path for air around it.

**The display readings.** Air-quality numbers and colour rings on the front are a pleasant convenience, nothing more. Don't pay extra for them.

## What to avoid

- **Buying one for a damp house.** No purifier cures damp. Damp is a ventilation and building problem — fix that, or you're running a fan at a wall that's failing anyway.
- **Expecting it to remove smells.** Odours are gases, not particles. Without a substantial activated carbon stage, last night's cooking smell will outlive the filter.
- **A room you never ventilate.** A purifier fighting a permanently sealed room is a losing battle. Open the windows sometimes regardless.
- **Filters you can't buy in the UK.** If replacements aren't stocked by UK sellers with prices shown, skip that machine entirely.
- **Health promises.** A purifier reduces your exposure to particles; it doesn't treat anything. If asthma or allergies are the reason you're shopping, your GP manages that — the purifier, at best, supports it.

## Bottom line

A purifier is worth it if your home has a specific problem: hay fever wrecking every spring, pets filling the air with dander, city traffic or a neighbour's wood burner pushing smoke through the window, or asthma and allergies where cleaner air genuinely helps — alongside your doctor's advice, not instead of it. It's a waste of money aimed at damp, at smells, or at a room nobody opens. For most people the honest answer is a mid-priced true HEPA unit, sized with room to spare, with filters that cost a sensible amount and are easy to find in the UK. That beats a big-brand £300 machine on a filter subscription you'll resent within a year.
