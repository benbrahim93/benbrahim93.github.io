The first properly cold morning, the bedroom window fogs over and the sills collect a puddle. By week three you're wiping glass with kitchen roll and eyeing the gadget aisle. Most of those boxes are the wrong first purchase; much of the real fix costs nothing.

Quick physics: warm indoor air carries moisture, and when it touches the coldest surface — usually the glass — it drops some of that water. Every fix either adds less moisture to the air or keeps surfaces from dumping it. Windows-only misting that clears by mid-morning is manageable; black patches on walls or ceilings that survive a wipe-down mean water sitting for hours, a bigger problem. Work down this ladder from free to expensive, and stop when the problem stops.

## How much should you actually spend?

**Step one: £0 — air and warmth.**

- Open the trickle vents, the little sliders along the top of many window frames; plenty of homes have them shut.
- Open a window fully for five minutes, morning and evening, rather than cracked all day — a sharp swap of air empties the moisture while losing little heat from the walls.
- Keep internal doors shut on damp rooms — bathroom after a shower, kitchen while cooking — so moisture stays where it's made.
- Deal with laundry, the biggest indoor water source: line-dry outside, or under covered ventilation. If it must dry indoors: one room, door shut, window open — no wet washing on radiators.
- Keep rooms gently heated rather than tropical-then-freezing: cold surfaces collect water, a steadily moderate room collects less.

**Step two: roughly a tenner — absorbers.** Tubs of crystals sold as condensation absorbers or moisture traps pull water from the air — roughly a cup over a damp week, then they want refilling. They earn their keep in enclosed spots: wardrobes, under-sink cupboards, the corner behind curtains. No tub out-drys a household that showers, cooks and breathes; they're for corners, not rooms.

**Step three: the real fix.** If the free fixes can't cope — ground-floor flat, windows shut all winter — a dehumidifier is the machine here that actually lowers room humidity. Size it to the space, set its humidistat moderate and let it switch itself on and off. Cost is the same sum as any heater: watts ÷ 1,000 × hours × your pence-per-kWh — read the rate off your current bill, since it moves. Park it wherever laundry dries and it tackles that moisture too.

**Step four: when it's the building.** Mould returning to the same patch despite all that, damp high on a wall, vents painted shut, seals crumbling, trouble worse after rain — stop buying products. Renters should report it in writing: landlords have legal duties around damp and mould, so photograph it, date it, keep the paper trail. Owners: an insulation or ventilation conversation.

## The features that matter

**Sizing the dehumidifier.** Match it to the room, not the house — a mid-sized unit handles a bedroom or living room. Check the tank; small tanks in damp rooms mean trudging to the sink twice a day. Compressor models are the common type and cheapest to run in a heated room; desiccant models cost slightly more to run but work better in cold rooms and blow out gently warmed air — good in garages and cold spare rooms.

**The humidistat.** A model that stops itself at a set humidity runs only as long as needed; one without hums all day and wastes money. It decides the bill. A laundry mode is a useful extra.

**Window vacuums.** Wet-vacs that gulp pooled water off the glass each morning. Convenience, nothing more — they shift no humidity, just turn the chore into two seconds.

**Mould sprays and anti-condensation paint.** A mould spray kills the surface patch; anti-condensation paint makes a cold wall slightly warmer so water forms less readily. Both hide the symptom — if the moisture stays, the black returns within the season. Last coat on a finished job, never the job.

## What to avoid

- **Buying the dehumidifier before the free fixes:** open vents, shut doors and moved laundry fix more wet winters than any gadget.
- **Expecting a tenner of crystals to solve a room.** They're for cupboards.
- **Sealing the house airtight.** Blocking the last airflow is how damp starts; you want controlled ventilation, not none.
- **Drying washing on radiators everywhere:** it dumps a day's water straight into the air, the fastest way to fog every pane.
- **Repainting over mould without touching the moisture.** It grows back through the paint.

## Bottom line

Condensation fixes stack, they don't swap. Open the vents and shut the doors first: free, and the genuine fix surprisingly often. Crystals for the stuffy corners. A dehumidifier when the room truly can't shed moisture, the humidistat keeping the bill sane. Sprays and paint as the last coat on a finished job, never the job.
